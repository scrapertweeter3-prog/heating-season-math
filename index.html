The heating season is short, so buy the sauna in April

A home sauna only earns money in the months a person turns it on. Suppose real use runs sixteen weeks a year, mid-November through early March, at four sessions a week: that is 64 paid-for sessions annually, and the other 36 weeks contribute zero no matter what the brochure promises about year-round ritual. Annualizing a purchase over 365 days when the habit lives in 112 of them is how gear budgets get flattered.

At 20 cents a kilowatt-hour, a 6-kilowatt unit drawing 7.5 kilowatt-hours per session costs $1.50 to run, so the season's electricity bill is $96. The unit itself, say $3,200 installed, is the whole decision. Spread the purchase over the ten seasons it will plausibly last and the true cost is $320 per season, or $5 per session at 64 sessions. Anyone comparing that $5 against a $35 recovery-lounge day pass should count their own calendar first: at two sessions a week the same purchase is $10 a session, and the lounge starts winning.

The calendar also sets the buy date. Ordering in April means the unit sits paid-off and idle through summer at zero marginal cost, arrives in fall with no rush premium, and misses the January queue entirely, when every other new-year buyer is waiting on the same installer. The HackedSelf review of home sauna running cost per session makes the same point from the other direction, and the site's protocol reviews keep dosing decisions tied to real schedules rather than ideal ones. Heat is a seasonal purchase with a session counter; budget it that way.
